## bendme

### A. Positioning

- User and situation: For MacBook owners who enjoy a playful desktop.
- Central promise: Lower your lid. Watch your desktop fold.
- Concrete experience: You gently lower the lid; the desktop bends with it. Open it again to return to the normal view.
- Payoff: The effect is the point. It adds a small moment of delight to a familiar machine.
- Reason to believe: BendMe’s public code, built-in artwork renders and the existing MacBook demo are available to inspect.
- Main objection: Requires macOS 14+, Apple silicon and a compatible lid sensor. Screen Recording permission is needed. Pause in Appearance or the menu bar.

### B. Three hero directions

1. **Lower your lid. Watch your desktop fold.** BendMe turns a small movement of your MacBook lid into a live visual effect. Choose a style, start the effect and watch your screen change perspective.
2. **A desktop that follows your MacBook lid.** You gently lower the lid; the desktop bends with it. Open it again to return to the normal view.
3. **A little lid movement. A visible bend.** A native Swift and Metal renderer uses screen capture and the lid sensor to draw the effect.

Recommended: direction 1. It gives the reader the clearest observable action and result at the scope supported by the project information. The other directions emphasize the recurring use situation and the product mechanism.

## resume-ai

### You know the work you’ve done. Let’s put it into words.

Resume AI is for those times when you sit down to write a resume and don’t know where to start.

You might have notes from different jobs, a few projects you want to mention, and an old resume that no longer says much about you. The idea behind Resume AI is to bring that experience together and help you turn it into a draft.

It uses AI-assisted writing to organize your work and skills. From there, you can read through the draft, change the wording and decide what matters for the role you’re applying for.

You still need to check that everything is true and sounds like you. The aim is to give you somewhere to start, without making up experience or promising an interview.

## yabs

### I wanted to compare servers without reading the logs over and over.

YABS gives you benchmark results. I built this visualizer to make those results easier to read when you’re choosing a VPS.

When you’re comparing servers, the specifications only tell you part of the story. I wanted to look at what the machines had actually measured, without going back and forth through terminal output.

The visualizer takes the results from YABS and presents them visually. You can look through the measurements and consider what they mean for the work you want the server to do.

A benchmark won’t tell you exactly how your own app will run. It gives you something to investigate. You can open the project or look through the source code below.

## arsenal

### I didn’t want useful prompts to get lost in old conversations.

Arsenal is a place to keep the instructions you want to come back to when you’re building with AI.

Sometimes you write a prompt that captures what you need really well. Then the conversation moves on, and the next time you need those instructions, you have to find them again.

That’s what I’m working on with Arsenal: keeping prompts and project context organized around the work they support. When a similar task comes up, you have a starting point you can return to and adapt.

You’ll still need to give the AI the right context and check what it produces. Having the instructions in one place just makes them easier to keep working with.

## scatter

### You drop a ball and wait to see where it lands.

That’s the idea behind Scatter. I wanted to see how much fun I could get out of a simple physics game.

You send a ball into the board and watch it bounce through the obstacles. The interesting part is following its path and wondering where the next bounce will take it.

I built the experiment around that anticipation: the fall, the collisions and the moment the ball lands. Then you can try another drop and watch what happens.

It’s a browser-game project I’m exploring. There isn’t a playable version linked here yet.

## laundirii

### A laundry order involves more than the person placing it.

LaunDiri is a team project about connecting the customer, the courier and the laundry shop.

Someone requests a laundry service, but there are other people involved in getting that order through. We wanted to make those handoffs part of the app from the beginning.

The Android project brings the customer’s request, the courier’s part and the shop’s part into one service workflow. We explored how those roles fit together as an order moves between them.

This is a project we built together. It isn’t an operating laundry service you can book through this page.

## techno-dynamic

### Sometimes a lesson leaves you with another question.

Techno Dynamic is a team project exploring how course material and AI-assisted content can work together in a technopreneurship course.

We wanted to bring the material a student is reading closer to the questions they might have about it. That became the starting point for this learning-platform project.

You can explore course material alongside generated content and suggestions based on frequently asked questions. The idea is to give you another way into the subject when you want to understand more.

Generated explanations still need checking. We explored a way to support learning; we haven’t established that it improves grades or replaces a teacher’s guidance.

## rad-ai

### I wanted to see what an ultrasound classifier returns.

Rad AI puts an image-classification experiment into an interface where you can inspect the model’s response.

A model is easier to explore when you can give it an input and see what comes back. With Rad AI, that input is a suitable ultrasound research image.

The interface connects the image to the classification model and shows its output. The project is about making that experimental process something you can inspect.

This is a research prototype. It hasn’t been clinically validated and must not be used to diagnose someone or make medical decisions.

## jungle-guardians

### What does a model see when you show it an animal photo?

Jungle Guardians is a learning project where you give a classifier an image and look at the category it predicts.

I wanted to make image classification easier to explore. An animal picture gives you a familiar starting point: you can look at the image yourself, then compare it with what the model says.

You choose an image, run it through the classifier and inspect the predicted category. That gives you a way to think about both the prediction and the mistakes a model can make.

It’s a learning project, so I wouldn’t treat the output as a reliable identification. I haven’t published an accuracy benchmark here.

## contact-manager

### Sometimes you just need to find someone’s details.

Contact Manager is a small project about keeping contact records organized and easy to return to.

I wanted to focus on the everyday work of looking through contacts and maintaining the information you’ve stored. That’s the scope of this app.

I used Angular for the interface and Supabase for the data side. The project centers on browsing and managing those records, so you have a place to come back to when you need the details.

I’m sharing it here as an application project. There isn’t a public version linked on this page.
